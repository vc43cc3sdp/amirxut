# Septic Tanks: A Comprehensive Guide for Homeowners

Skip to content iStock

More than 25% of Americans use a septic tank to manage waste, especially in rural areas. Having a septic system comes with responsibilities, including knowing how the septic system works, maintaining it properly, and recognizing when it needs attention. This comprehensive guide covers everything you need to know about septic tanks, from their basic functionality to types, maintenance requirements, and associated costs. Related All About Plumbing What is a Septic Tank?

A septic tank is a watertight receptacle that’s buried in the ground. A two- to three-bedroom house usually requires a minimum of a 1000-gallon capacity tank. It’s connected to the house’s waste drains by a pipe that feeds into the tank inlet. Partial walls inside the tank known as baffles are meant to promote the settling of waste. Although older tanks may not have them, most new tanks are fitted with filters that prevent smaller particles from entering the drain field. Tanks also often feature a port or riser at the top for pumping access.

Pro Tip: Rick Peer, septic system installer featured on This Old House, explains how a two-compartment tank works: “This first compartment…this is the trash side of the tank, so pretty much everything from the home will drop into this first compartment. The solids will either float to the top or settle out and sink to the bottom, leaving a dirty zone of water in the center. That water will pass through into this second compartment through a baffle wall.” The baffle opening sits about 22 to 24 inches off the bottom of the tank, allowing only the middle layer of water to pass through. How Do Septic Tanks Work?

Waste flows from the house to the tank, where heavier solids (sludge) settle to the tank’s bottom while lighter solids and liquids remain at the top. In the tank, colonies of bacteria break down the sewage. The waste liquids (effluent) flow out of the tank and down into the drain or leach field via perforated pipes laid in trenches filled with gravel or stone and covered with geo-fabric and dirt. The stone/gravel filters the effluent, which then drains into the soil below, where it is processed by microbes that live in the soil, and finally flows as clean water into the groundwater. Components of a Septic Tank System

An effective septic system contains several components that work in unison to process household waste. Septic Tank

This is where the initial treatment takes place. Solids settle at the bottom (sludge), and oils and grease float to the top (scum). Naturally occurring bacteria decompose the solid matter. Piping

Piping is a crucial aspect of your septic system. Inlet and outlet pipes transfer wastewater into and out of the tank. Leach pipes distribute effluent through the drain field.

Inlet pipe: Wastewater from your home enters the septic tank through an inlet pipe

Outlet pipe: The treated liquid, or effluent, flows out of the septic tank and into the drain field Distribution Box

This component evenly distributes wastewater from the septic tank to the drain field lines. If it becomes clogged or damaged, it can lead to uneven distribution and system failure. Drain Field

Also known as a leach field, this is where the wastewater undergoes further treatment by filtering through soil. The soil’s natural filtering capability removes harmful substances from the effluent before it reenters the groundwater system. Types of Septic Tanks

Septic systems come in different types, each suited to various needs and ground conditions. Knowing the type of septic system you have can help you maintain it properly. Anaerobic Systems

Anaerobic systems are the most common type used in residential properties. These systems work without oxygen. Wastewater moves into the tank, and anaerobic bacteria break down the solids. The entire process is relatively simple and less costly to install compared to other types. Aerobic Systems

Aerobic systems are more complex than anaerobic systems and use oxygen to treat wastewater. Because they introduce air into the tank, aerobic systems can handle and treat waste more efficiently. They’re suitable for properties with poor soil conditions or smaller drain fields but are generally more costly to install and maintain.

Pro Tip: As This Old House’s Richard Trethewey explains, the key difference comes down to oxygen: “Bacteria loves oxygen. So we have an air pump that’s going to aerate the bottom of this and break down those solids.” In aerobic systems, compressors actively feed oxygen to bacteria, dramatically speeding up the breakdown of waste compared to passive anaerobic tanks.

Materials Used in Septic Tank Construction

The best material for a septic tank depends on various factors, including cost, local regulations, and ground conditions. Here are the most commonly used materials:

Concrete: Concrete tanks are the most traditional option and are liked for their durability. They’re less likely to “float” in upended soil conditions but can crack under extreme pressure or shifting.

Fiberglass: Fiberglass tanks are lightweight and resistant to cracking, rusting, and biological buildup. They’re easy to transport and install, though they may shift in certain soil conditions.

Polyethylene: Polyethylene tanks offer a lightweight, durable option that is resistant to cracking and rusting. They’re less prone to leaking and are easier to install in remote or difficult-to-access areas.

Pro Tip: Richard Trethewey cautions that lightweight tanks come with a hidden risk: if buried in the ground, they can float away. As he noted during a visit to a septic research facility, a 1,000-gallon tank that shifts underground could be “moving towards your neighbor’s house”—a serious problem that makes proper anchoring essential for fiberglass and polyethylene installations.

Maintenance Requirements and Frequency for a Septic Tank

Proper maintenance extends the life of your septic system and ensures it operates efficiently.

This Old House Magazine contributor Max Alexander learned the hard way what happens when a septic system is neglected. After black gunk started oozing up from the lawn of his 1850 Maine farmhouse, he discovered the decades-old system had failed and needed full replacement. The chief cause of leach field failure? Neglecting to remove grease and solids from the tank every few years. Experts say tank-pumping is the only maintenance chore most septic systems need—and that additives supposedly promoting good bacteria are unnecessary and possibly harmful. Regular Inspections

The EPA (Environmental Protection Agency) recommends that septic tanks be inspected every three years by a professional. To find wastewater professionals in your area, check out The National Onsite Wastewater Recycling Association (NOWRA). Mechanical parts like pumps and switches should be inspected annually. Pumping Frequency

Septic tanks need to be pumped to remove the buildup of scum and sludge. If your drains are slowing down or you have a new pond in your backyard, it may be a sign that your tank needs to be pumped.

Pro Tip: This Old House master carpenter Norm Abram, who has a gravity-based septic system at his home in Massachusetts, advises: “It is really important that people get their tanks pumped out frequently. I try to do mine every two years. It’s short money for what you end up getting.” Water Conservation

Conserving water reduces the load on your septic tank. Fix leaks, install low-flow fixtures, and avoid running multiple water-heavy appliances simultaneously to reduce the strain on your system. Proper Waste Disposal

Avoid flushing nonbiodegradable items, grease, or harsh chemicals down the drain. These can damage your system or block pipes, leading to costly repairs.

Regulations and Permits for Installing a Septic Tank

Compliance with local regulations and obtaining the necessary permits is critical when installing or modifying a septic system. Federal Guidelines

The EPA offers guidelines on septic system management. These guidelines aim to protect public health and the environment by ensuring systems are designed, installed, and maintained properly. State and Local Regulations

State and local regulations vary but generally include specific requirements for system design, installation, and maintenance. Always consult local authorities to ensure compliance. Permitting Process
