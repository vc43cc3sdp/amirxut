The law requires homeowners to add an egress to their basements as points of exit in potential emergencies. There are many models from which homeowners can choose, such as bulkheads, full-height entrances, or window wells, and each has its own merits and disadvantages. Read more about different types of basement egress below, and start planning for installation, whether you’re finishing your basement or simply want to improve your emergency exit options. Do You Need a Specialist?

Most licensed general contractors can install egress and obtain the necessary permits. The contractor you hire may need to collaborate with the professionals below:

An engineer to design structural modifications

A landscape contractor for yard excavation

A masonry contractor to cut the foundation and potentially add concrete stairs

Experts can help you to navigate the permit process, and make sure you abide by local regulations.

Egress Windows: Best for Basements Partially Above Grade

The sill below these 21-by-40-inch egress casements sits 40 inches above the basement floor. Outside, the window openings are 11 inches above grade. SHOWN: Envision casement windows; vectorwindows.com | Hannah Burkett

Egress windows are an excellent choice for basements that are partially above grade. They serve as emergency exits and allow natural light to enter the space. Pros of an Egress Window

Egress windows offer several advantages: Increased natural light in the basement Improved ventilation

Relatively simple installation, especially if you’re replacing existing utility windows in a basement

They don’t require a new header installation Cons of an Egress Window

There are a few potential drawbacks to consider, including those below.

Can allow water in if you don’t install it correctly

May require a window well, which increases complexity and cost

Not useful if you’re moving large items in and out of the basement What’s Involved

Follow the steps below to install an egress window that’s safe and weatherproof.

Cut a rough opening in the foundation or wall.

Patch and prepare the edges of the opening.

Install a window buck for proper anchoring. Fit and secure the window.

Flash and trim the window according to manufacturer instructions.

When fitting the window, apply a bead of caulk around only three sides of the opening—not the bottom. As This Old House general contractor Sean explained during a recent project, “We’re not gonna caulk the bottom because, again, back to that water migration, we wanna give a gap for that water to get out from underneath.” A composite shim lifts the window about a quarter inch off the sill, giving any water that enters the system a clear path to escape. Consider the variables below when you’re selecting an egress window. You should also consider your aesthetic and functional needs.

Casement windows that open 90 degrees are frequently the best choice

Composite or fiberglass frames are especially moisture-resistant

Double-hung and sliding windows may need to be larger to meet code requirements

Tilt-and-turn windows offer versatility for ventilation and egress Total Cost of an Egress Window

Installing an egress window can cost between $2,712 and $5,824, depending on factors such as foundation type, window style, and any necessary structural modifications. Accounting for these variables will help you to budget accurately.

Window Wells: Best for Egress Windows Below Grade

If the egress window sits below grade in a basement, you’ll need a window well. This excavated area offers access to the window and can make the basement more comfortable.

Pro Tip: Mark, a This Old House contractor, explains that window wells serve multiple purposes: “One of the things they do is they’re going to help mitigate the water. They’re going to keep pests out. But they also let in light. It protects the window. I think that’s most important to me.”

On a recent This Old House project, contractor Mike opted for a Bilco escape well over traditional pressure-treated timber retaining walls around the basement window. As he explained, the benefits are significant: “We’re getting a much bigger retaining structure to allow more light into the basement area. This happens to be the portion of the basement that will be finished. So bigger, more natural light goes together a lot faster than using pressure treated timbers. It also is code compliant for egress situations.” Pros of a Window Well

To secure the window well to the foundation, you’ll need a hammer drill fitted with a masonry bit to bore into the concrete. As This Old House’s Mark demonstrates, the well is then fastened using expanding wedge bolts—once the nut is tightened with a ratchet, an internal sleeve expands and grabs inside the drilled hole, creating a permanent anchor. Plan on six bolts per well for a secure attachment. Code Requirements

Window wells must have the following features to serve a safe and effective escape routes: A cover for safety

Built-in steps or ladder if they’re deeper than 44 inches

Minimum clear area of 9 square feet for escape

Must be at least 36 inches from the foundation wall

Window well extensions come in standard depths—typically 12-inch, 18-inch, and 24-inch options. As Mark explains, the depth you choose depends on how much light exposure you need at the window. A shallower 12-inch extension works where the window sits closer to grade, while an 18- or 24-inch depth is necessary for deeper wells. Always measure from flange to flange and establish a center line on the window to ensure the well is properly aligned and balanced—an off-center well can cause drainage and structural issues over time. Incorporate plantings to improve the view and create a more natural appearance.

Use materials such as cedar timbers or precast concrete blocks for a unique look.

Include a few feet of gravel at the base of the well.

Install a drainpipe that’s connected to the foundation drain or a dry well. Total Cost of a Window Well

Installing a window well for a below-ground egress basement window adds about $50–$200 per cubic yard in excavation costs, plus another $50 to $300 for permits. A professional land excavator typically charges between $50 and $200 per cubic yard to install your window well. You may also need a window well cover to prevent soil moisture build-up and keep pests and bugs out of your basement, which will cost around $600.

Accessible for people with mobility concerns

Enhance the utility of a basement environment

Follow the steps below to install a bulkhead door:

Excavate the area for the stairs and landing.

Cut an opening into the foundation wall.

Install a header or lintel above the opening. Frame the opening with bucks.

Install precast concrete stairs or pour them on-site. Mount and seal the bulkhead door unit. Code Requirements

Bulkhead doors must meet the following standards for code compliance: Adequate lighting for safety

At least as wide as any door at the bottom of the stairs

Easy to open from the inside without keys or tools

Proper drainage to prevent water accumulation Good to Know

Full-Height Entrance with Stairs: Best for a Separate Apartment

Exterior-grade doors and masonry walls and walkways make daily comings and goings easier. To keep water from collecting outside the entrance, this setup usually requires tying into existing drainage or adding a dry well. | Courtesy CL Design-Build

A full-height entrance with stairs is ideal for basements that you’ve converted into separate living spaces, such as rental units, in-law apartments, or home offices. Pros of a Full-Height Entrance

Full-height entrances offer several benefits, including the following:

Allow easy access for people and large items Can include windows for natural light Include a separate mailing address Cons of a Full-Height Entrance

Full-height entrances may include the drawbacks below.

Require significant yard space (30 square feet or more)

May need to be on the front or side of the house

May require zoning variances in some areas

More expensive than other egress options What’s Involved

Follow the steps below to install a full-height entrance: Excavate the yard area.

Cut a full-size door opening in the foundation.

Install a lintel to support the house above the opening.

Frame the opening and install an exterior-grade door.

Pour concrete stairs or install precast steps.

Add proper drainage and waterproofing measures. Code Requirements

Full-height entrances must meet the specific requirements below. These will keep your entrance safe, accessible, and fully compliant with building regulations:

Entry door must be 36 inches wide and 80 inches tall.

Guardrails and handrails must have four steps or more. Stairs must be at least 36 inches wide.

The landing at the base of the stairs must be 6 inches below the basement floor level.

Treads must be 11 inches deep, and risers should not be more than 7 3/4 inches tall. Good to Know Ian Worpole

Consider the variables below when you’re planning a full-height entrance:

Adding a roof or awning can help to better protect against the elements.

Adequate drainage can prevent water intrusion.

Installing footings under the walls can prevent frost heaving in cold climates.

Proper lighting offers safety and convenience. Total Cost of a Full-Height Entrance
