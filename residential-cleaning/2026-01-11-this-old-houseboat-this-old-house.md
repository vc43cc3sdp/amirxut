In 2001, after deciding to start a family, my wife, Krissy, and I realized that our 1,390-square-foot one-bedroom houseboat on Seattle’s Lake Union wouldn’t be big enough for us for long. We decided to add on and reconfigure the inefficient layout, which required us to walk through four rooms to get from the main entrance to the home’s only bathroom. Seattle laws limit the height of floating homes to 18 feet off the water, but I didn’t want some boxy structure with low ceilings just to get extra square footage. I fought to maintain the look and feel of the original gabled structure. I decided to keep the south half of the house, with the living room, dining room, and one of the home’s two upstairs sleeping lofts, largely intact. At the north end of the house, I planned to raise the roof and add a gabled dormer to fit a full-fledged master suite upstairs and a bedroom downstairs. The two rebuilt bedrooms and a new bath added only 30 square feet of space but helped preserve the houseboat’s gabled charm.

Before tackling the redesign, homeowner Todd Biernacki spent years preparing—”teaching myself to use a CAD program, and consulting my old boss, contractor Eric Hogeboom,” he recalls. “All that up-front work paid off when a local engineering firm and the building department signed off on my design for our renovated home.” Houseboat Homeowners with Their Kids Photo by Alex Hayden

I had spent nine months working with a contracting firm called Envirotecture, which specializes in houseboats, but I’d never done any designing. So I spent months doing research, teaching myself to use a CAD program, and consulting my old boss, contractor Eric Hogeboom. All that work paid off when an engineering firm and the building department signed off on my design. The addition’s extra weight meant I’d need more flotation. Our houseboat was built on a raft of old-growth cedar logs, but after a century underwater, they weren’t very bouyant. About 85 steel drums and some Styrofoam had been added over time, but it was all poorly placed, and the barrels were rusting apart. So, armed with scuba gear, I removed this extra flotation from half of the house. I did several dives to add and move around 80 new 55-gallon plastic barrels, recycled from a local soda-bottling plant.

The challenge, as the homeowner explained, was balancing efficiency with character: “Seattle laws limit the height of floating homes to 18 feet off the water and rarely allow for larger footprints, but I didn’t want to end up with some boxy structure with low ceilings just to get extra square footage. So when planning out the new design, I fought to maintain the look and feel of the original gabled structure.”

Building square and plumb on water meant rethinking the basics. “When working on water, levels are useless except as straightedges, so I ran strings to make sure the floor framing stayed in plane,” the homeowner explained. To keep the house from twisting—or listing so badly that doors and drawers opened on their own—he added stringers of pressure-treated 6x8s running perpendicular to the logs, pinning them with galvanized steel bolts and bearing plates. Entry Area Photo by Alex Hayden

To make the house as eco-friendly as possible, we used recycled and salvaged pieces when we could. For the flooring in the entry and living areas, Krissy got her hands on some old maple from a circa-1865 linen mill in North Carolina. It was only $3.60 per square foot. Even at that price, I almost sent it back. It looked terrible, with cracks, splits, metal embedded in many boards, and lots of holes. After installing the better boards, I spent two days filling something like 2,000 original nail holes with epoxy. But once it was sanded and finished it was stunning. Kitchen Photo by Alex Hayden

The flooring and peninsula in the kitchen are also reclaimed: teak plants from a mid-19th-century Dutch colonial building in Indonesia. We happened to find the exotic boards in the back room of a local furniture importer. The refinished shell of an antique wood-burning range encases a new glass cooktop. A black stovepipe masks the ducting that leads to a vent fan in the attic. The 5-foot salvaged sink came from a local hotel that was condemned after an earthquake. I saved about $10,000 by buying off-white, stock kitchen cabinets at Home Depot, then tricking them out with extra trim and matching paint.

The whole family enjoys peering through the inch-thick, 2-foot-square Plexiglas window in the floor outside the first-floor bath. There we can view the houseboat’s original timber raft, as well as crawdads that scuttle over it and fish that swim in the waters below. The Deck Photo by Alex Hayden

I gave similar attention to the exterior, laying out sustainable Brazilian redwood decking, creating a floating garden with built-in planters and a two-person swing, then hand-weaving copper tubing into a curvy, whimsical railing. It helps keep Devon, now 3, and his 8-month-old sister, Sophie, safely away from the Lake Union waters that we all love so much. What I Did Photo by Alex Hayden Renovated a century-old houseboat. Remodeling cost: $250,000 Time frame: 4 years

Where I saved: Used salvaged materials and did most of the work myself.

Where I splurged: Wood windows, doors, and trim that I had to sand, stain, and finish.

What I’d do differently: Not use sanded glass bath tile. It’s beautiful, but though I pretreated with a sealer before grouting, I had to spend three days with a random-orbit sander taking off a grout haze of silicone caulk that remained.

Biggest challenge: Trying to build a square, level, and plumb addition without being able to use two normally indispensable carpenter tools: a level and a plumb bob.

How I solved it: Running lots of string lines and retaking lots of measurements.
