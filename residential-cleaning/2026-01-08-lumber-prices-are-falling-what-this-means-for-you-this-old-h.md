# Lumber Prices Are Falling: What This Means for You - This Old House

Skip to content iStock

Over the past two years, walking through the lumber rack at a local home improvement store elicited shaking heads and murmurs about the way things used to be. Throughout the Covid-19 pandemic, lumber prices rose to almost four times their pre-COVID prices. And while these prices have changed the landscape of the home improvement, home building, renovation, and real estate industries, things are changing once more. Lumber prices are decreasing.

But when can consumers expect to see the price drop reflected in the aisle with 2x4s and plywood? And what does it mean for those industries so affected? Keep reading to find out. What Caused the Lumber Price Spike?

Before discussing the falling prices, it’s important to note why they jumped during the pandemic. And the factors are many:

Lumber production companies reduced production or closed due to local regulations regarding the pandemic, diminishing the supply.

Many lumber producers believed the demand for lumber would plummet due to suspected construction slowdowns.

DIYers forced to stay home undertook renovations and projects at incredible rates to pass the time, purchasing lumber at higher rates than expected and increasing demand.

People fleeing cities for wide-open spaces were building new homes and renovating fixer-uppers.

All of these factors combined to increase the cost of lumber from pre-pandemic levels of just over $400 per thousand board feet (the unit of measurement used for wood) to over $1,500 in early to mid-2021, according to Statista. These prices fluctuated back and forth before reaching the 2022 high of around $1,300 over the early months of 2022.

Pro Tip: The price volatility also highlighted how much geography affects lumber costs. On a This Old House project build, the crew discovered that an 8-foot two-by-four spruce that cost roughly $2.60 back home could run around $6.50 in remote locations—and a three-quarter-inch sheet of CDX plywood could jump from about $24 to $65, in part because of import tolls as high as 33% on incoming goods. These regional disparities only worsened when national supply chains tightened during the pandemic. Lumber Costs Impact Building Costs

The National Association of Home Builders estimates that 9 out of 10 single-family homes built in the U.S. feature wood-framed walls, ceilings, floors, and roofs. With the average cost of 1,000 board feet spiking up to four times pre-pandemic levels, it’s estimated that the average cost to build a home jumped over $18,000. Considering that the framing package for the average single-family home cost between $30,000 and $40,000, this is a significant jump.

It wasn’t just new home building, though. Structural renovations, DIY projects, and other uses for framing lumber, hardwood, and plywood skyrocketed in cost.

Rate Hikes and a Slowing Housing Market are Squashing Lumber Prices

While the sudden spike in prices may have been historic, the cost of lumber is now starting to decline. With the federal government’s goal of slowing inflation came higher interest rates, which in turn priced some homebuyers out of the market. Homes they could once afford are now out of their price range, leaving more inventory on the block and lessening the need for new construction.

With the relative slowdown in the real estate market, supply is finally beginning to match demand. Wood isn’t flying off the lumberyard shelves like it once was. Without the insatiable demand for lumber, prices will start to drop—though it may be a while before the end consumer sees it.

Near Pre-Pandemic Lumber Prices are Possible — But Not Lower

While the lumber prices are dropping, they aren’t in freefall. Experts expect lumber prices to decline gradually over the next few months. As the prices stabilize, they should land between $450 and $600 per thousand board feet, which is still more than the cost of lumber two and half years ago, but a far cry from the $1,500 price per thousand board feet. As of the time of this writing, it’s hovering around $646.

It might seem that there will be a direct correlation between the falling lumber prices and the price of building new homes dropping, but that’s not the case—at least not yet. The number of project starts far outweighs the number of project completions at this point, which means that lumber is still in demand for contractors. But that won’t last forever.

Lumberyards are beginning to cut prices and production as materials are starting to pile up. As higher interest rates stabilize the market and more projects come to completion, the demand for that lumber should begin to fall off.

Also, it may be too soon to tell how lower lumber prices will affect the real estate market. Despite the higher interest rates, housing is still in relatively high demand across the country. If the number of project starts slows to match the rates of completion, there will be fewer available homes on the market. This could insulate the inflated prices of existing homes. Those insulated prices coupled with higher rates could make it even harder for first-time home buyers to purchase.

What Does Falling Lumber Prices Mean for DIYers and Home Improvement?

Regarding DIY and home improvement, it may be a while before consumers see lumber prices actually bottom out and stabilize. While the average cost of 1,000 board feet may be dropping, those costs have to pass through several stops in the supply chain before they hit the shelves of lumberyards and home improvement stores. Understanding seasonal trends in lumber pricing can help consumers and builders make more informed purchasing decisions. Best Times to Buy Lumber

Historically, lumber prices tend to be lower during the winter months when construction activity slows. However, recent market volatility has disrupted some of these traditional patterns. Factors Affecting Seasonal Pricing

Several factors influence seasonal lumber pricing, including:

Weather conditions affecting harvesting and transportation Construction industry demand cycles Global economic trends

Environmental and Sustainability Considerations for Lumber

As lumber prices fluctuate, it’s important to consider the environmental and sustainability factors associated with lumber production and consumption. Sustainable forest management practices and certifications such as FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) can ensure that the lumber used in construction and DIY projects comes from responsibly managed sources.

Pro Tip: This Old House master carpenter Norm Abram has long advocated for engineered lumber and durable alternatives. Engineered lumber—wood chips and veneers glued up to make studs, rafters, and joists—”performs more predictably than sawn, and comes from young trees in farmed forests, a resource largely free of the restrictions and anti-logging animosity surrounding old timber stands,” according to Norm. He adds candidly: “As much as I love wood, it’s not the best choice for a building material. It just doesn’t survive outside in the long run.” Sustainable Forest Management

Exploring alternative building materials can be beneficial. Materials such as steel, concrete, and engineered wood products offer different advantages and price stability. The demand for lumber can have significant environmental impacts, including deforestation and habitat destruction. Homeowners and DIY enthusiasts can also make eco-friendly choices by repurposing salvaged wood and using reclaimed materials.

Technological Advancements in the Lumber Industry

Technological advancements are transforming the lumber industry, leading to greater efficiency and potential cost savings. These innovations can also impact lumber prices and availability in the market. Automation and AI

The adoption of automation and artificial intelligence (AI) in lumber production is streamlining processes and reducing labor costs. Automated machinery can increase production speed, improve precision, and enhance overall efficiency. These advancements can contribute to more stable lumber prices and reduce dependence on manual labor. Improved Harvesting Techniques

New harvesting techniques and equipment are making it easier to manage forests sustainably. Innovations such as selective logging and remote sensing technology allow for more precise and less invasive harvesting. These methods can help maintain forest health and ensure a steady supply of lumber. Enhanced Supply Chain Management

Technological advancements are also improving supply chain management in the lumber industry. Real-time tracking and inventory management systems enable better coordination and reduce delays. This can help stabilize prices by ensuring a more consistent supply of lumber products. Our Conclusion

The falling lumber prices signal a positive shift for the construction industry, homeowners, and DIY enthusiasts. While prices may not return to pre-pandemic levels, the current downward trend offers relief from the extreme highs experienced in recent years.

As the market continues to stabilize, consumers should see gradual improvements in pricing for lumber-related products and projects. However, it’s important to remember that lumber is just one component of construction and renovation costs. Other factors, such as labor shortages and overall economic conditions, will continue to play significant roles in project planning and budgeting.
