# Drilling Pilot Holes: A Comprehensive Guide for Your Projects - This Old House

Skip to content

Drilling pilot holes is a technique in woodworking and construction that can significantly improve the quality and durability of your projects. Whether you’re hanging shelves, assembling furniture, or working on a major renovation, knowing how to properly drill pilot holes can prevent wood splitting, help with accurate screw placement, and create stronger joints.

In this guide, we’ll explore the importance of pilot holes, the tools you’ll need, and step-by-step instructions for drilling perfect pilot holes every time. What Are Pilot Holes?

Pilot holes are small holes drilled into a material before inserting screws or nails. They help to prevent wood from splitting, especially when you’re working near the edges of boards or with hardwoods. They also guide screws into the correct position, promoting straight and accurate fastening and minimizing the risk of stripping screw heads.

Necessary Tools for Drilling Pilot Holes

In addition to a pencil and safety gear, you’ll need a few key tools:

Drill bits: For woodworking, brad-point bits are ideal as they create clean, precise holes. If you’re working with metal or plastic, choose specialized drill bits for those materials.

Hand tools: For delicate work or tight spaces, a hand drill can be useful when a power drill is too bulky. Push drills are also an option for precision work.

When starting a pilot hole, switch the speed range to low for increased torque, and start slowly before increasing speed once the bit is on its way.

How To Choose the Correct Pilot Hole Size

Picking the correct size for your pilot hole is key to the strength of your project. Several factors can influence the ideal hole size:

Wood hardness: Harder woods require larger pilot holes to prevent splitting.

Material: Various materials require different sizes.

Screw size: Larger screws need bigger pilot holes.

Proximity to edges: Holes near the edge of a board may need to be slightly smaller to maintain strength.

Project requirements: Some projects may require tighter or looser fits, depending on their purpose.

Pro Tip: Even with a proper-size pilot hole, it can be difficult to drive a screw into some hardwoods. A little beeswax on the screw threads works wonders—just be careful to keep flecks of wax off the surface if the wood will be stained or clear-finished. Don’t use soap; it attracts moisture, which can cause rust.

Pilot Holes Sizes for Different Materials

Understanding how materials react to pilot holes is important for reducing damage and getting the best results:

Wood: Your pilot hole should be slightly smaller than the diameter of the screw’s shaft (not including the threads) to allow the screw threads to bite into the wood. For softwoods, aim for a pilot hole about 90% of the screw’s shaft diameter. For hardwoods, you can go up to 95%.

Metal: Metal pilot holes often need to be the exact size of the screw’s minor diameter (the diameter at the base of the threads). Use a drill bit designed for metal, use cutting oil, work slowly, and apply steady pressure to reduce friction and heat.

Drywall: For drywall, pilot holes are often unnecessary unless you’re working near the edge. If needed, use a bit slightly smaller than the drywall anchor you plan to use and proceed with caution to avoid damaging the drywall.

Masonry: Drilling pilot holes in masonry, such as brick or concrete, requires specialized tools and techniques, including carbide or diamond-tipped masonry drill bits and a hammer drill.

Step-by-Step Guide To Drilling Pilot Holes

Follow these simple steps to drill perfect pilot holes: 1. Mark the Spot

Start by clearly marking where you want to drill your pilot hole. Use a pencil or awl to make a small indentation, which will help guide your drill bit. 2. Select the Right Drill Bit

[Old House](https://old-house.swapstreet.shop/old-house/20260822329.html)

Choose a drill bit that’s slightly smaller than your screw’s shaft diameter. If you’re unsure, err on the side of a smaller bit—you can always make the hole larger if necessary.

Pro Tip: To match a bit to a straight-shank screw, line up the bit and screw with the bit closer to you and hold them up to the light. The bit is the right size if it just covers the shank of the screw while the screw’s threads remain visible on either side. For screws with tapered shanks, use a tapered pilot bit with an integral countersink, which digs a cone-shaped recess for the screwhead and can be adjusted to suit screws of different lengths. 3. Set Up Your Drill

Insert the chosen bit into your drill and tighten the chuck securely. If your drill has multiple speed settings, start with a lower speed for better control. 4. Use the Drill

Place the drill bit on your marked spot, keeping the drill perpendicular to the surface. Apply steady pressure and start drilling slowly, increasing speed as the bit penetrates the material. Drill slightly deeper than the length of the screw.

Pro Tip: Norm Abram recommends always drilling a little deeper than the length of the screw: “If you don’t, the screw will bottom out before it is fully driven in and the last few turns will be tough, particularly in hardwood. And when you apply more pressure, there’s a greater chance that you’ll mangle the screwhead or scratch your workpiece.” To track your depth, wrap the bit with a piece of blue painter’s tape right at the target depth.

Advanced Techniques for Drilling Pilot Holes

Once you’ve mastered the basics, try these advanced techniques to improve your pilot hole drilling: Use a Nail as a Drill Bit

[Guide for Your Projects](https://guide-for-your-projects.curblist.xyz/guide-for-your-projects/20260822.html)

In a pinch, a finishing nail can serve as a makeshift bit for small holes. Chuck the nail into your drill, and use light pressure to avoid bending it. This technique is great for thin workpieces or when you don’t have the exact size drill bit on hand.

The Finishing-Nail Trick: This technique comes straight from the TOH Magazine workshop: when finish-nailing into a tough hardwood such as oak, the chances are good that the nail will bend or split the wood unless you drill a pilot hole first. Instead of searching for a bit that’s not too small and not too big, take a finishing nail, snip off its head with a wire cutter, and chuck the shaft into your drill. The nail’s faceted point will bore through the wood, leaving a perfectly sized pilot hole for another nail. Opt for a Drill Press

Another option for accurate holes is a drill press, which offers greater control over the drilling process. This tool is especially useful for cabinetry or intricate woodworking projects.

To use a drill press for pilot holes, follow these steps:

Set the drill press to the correct height and secure your workpiece.

Select the appropriate drill bit and install it in the chuck.

Align the drill bit with your marked spot on the workpiece.

Lower the drill bit slowly, applying steady pressure to create the pilot hole. Countersink for Flush Screws

For a professional finish, try countersinking your pilot holes. This creates a recess for the screw head, allowing it to sit flush with or below the surface of the wood. Use a countersink bit or a combination drill and countersink bit for efficiency.

Pro Tip: When countersinking pilot holes, consider investing in a tapered drill bit with an integral countersink. As shown on This Old House, tapered bits match the shape of a wood screw’s shank, so the screw holds better in the wood. A small set screw on the bit lets you adjust how much the bit projects past the countersink to suit screws of different lengths—making one bit versatile enough for multiple screw sizes.

Common Mistakes To Avoid When Drilling Pilot Holes

For the best results, avoid these common pitfalls:

Using the wrong size bit: Too large a bit can lead to loose screws, while too small can cause splitting.

Drilling too deep: This can weaken the material and reduce the screw’s holding power.

Not drilling straight: Angled pilot holes can lead to misaligned screws and weak joints.

Forgetting to account for materials: Different woods and materials require various approaches.

Skipping pilot holes altogether: This increases the risk of splitting and makes driving screws more difficult. Safety Tips for Drilling Pilot Holes

Safety should always be a priority when working with power tools. Follow these precautions:

Wear safety glasses to protect your eyes from debris.

Use work gloves to guard against splinters and sharp edges.

Secure your workpiece to prevent slipping. Keep your work area clean and well-lit.

Unplug your drill when changing bits or making adjustments.

Pro Tip: Tom Silva, This Old House general contractor, always inspects his bits before drilling: “If it’s not in good shape, I won’t use it,” he says. Whenever he picks up a bit, he looks for nicks, rounded edges, and the blue discoloration that indicates lost temper, then grazes his fingertips over the edges to test sharpness.

Troubleshooting Common Pilot Hole Problems

If you encounter issues while drilling pilot holes, try these solutions:

The hole is too large: Use a wooden dowel and wood glue to fill the hole, then redrill.

Splintering: Place masking tape over the drill site before drilling.

Off-center holes: Use a drill press or guide for more precise alignment.

Overheating: Reduce drill speed and pressure, and allow the bit to cool between holes.
